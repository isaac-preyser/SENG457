Classical vs. Quantum Computing¶

Quantum computing is a completely different model of computation.

A key difference is that qubits can be in superposition- that is, data can exist on a spectrum anywhere from $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{1}$.

It is based on linear algebra, rather than boolean algebra in the classical realm. States may be ambiguous, due to entanglement and importantly, interference.

Noise can often be characterized statistically by running a circuit many times and sampling, but interference itself is something we intentionally engineer inside the algorithm.

Quantum algorithms explore superposed computational paths and then use interference to bias the final measurement outcomes. The gates themselves are unitary and deterministic; the randomness appears when we measure.

Basic Gates¶

$\ket{\phi}$ Gate: Initializes qubit to a given state, described by $\phi$.

Note that this gate is not physically realizable on real quantum hardware, but is useful for simulation.

Hadamard (H) Gate: Puts a qubit into superposition. Note the definition of Hadamard at IBM Quantum Learning

$$ H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Note: The Hadamard gate is a LINEAR operator. Therefore we can take advantage of the properties of linearity, which is especially useful when dealing with states in superposition.

CNOT: Depending on the value of the control-bit, this will perform a NOT operation (Pauli-X matrix) on the target qubit.

Measurement: Collapses the qubit and measures it, and outputs to a classical bit.

Matrix Notation¶

Dirac notation is a way of doing "short-hand" for large vectors. This simplifies working with matrices.

Bra-Ket notation is the standard way of communicating Quantum Information.

Computational Basis¶

In quantum mechanics, $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ are the fundamental, orthogonal states of a qubit, analogous to $0$ and $1$ of a classical bit.

They form the building blocks for any more complex quantum state.

$$ \ket{0} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \ket{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

So what is a Bra and a Ket?¶

A "Ket" $\ket{\psi}$ is a column vector, representing a quantum state. A "Bra" $\bra{\psi}$ is the conjugate transpose of a Ket, represented as a row vector.

The Ket captures all information about a quantum state, while the Bra is used in operations like inner products and outer products.

Conjugate Transpose¶

The conjugate transpose (or Hermitian transpose, notated by $\dagger$) of a matrix is obtained by taking the transpose of the matrix and then taking the complex conjugate of each element — that is, changing the sign of the imaginary part.

To do this, replace every complex number in the vector with it's complex conjugate (i.e., change the sign of the imaginary part), then transpose the vector (i.e., convert rows to columns and vice versa).

$$ \text{If } \ket{\psi} = \begin{bmatrix} a + ib \\ c + id \end{bmatrix}, \text{ then } \bra{\psi} = \begin{bmatrix} a - ib & c - id \end{bmatrix} $$

Inner Product¶

The inner product (or dot product) of two quantum states $\ket{\phi}$ and $\ket{\psi}$ is denoted as $\langle \phi | \psi \rangle$.

It is calculated like a dot product, and results in a scalar.

These products are often referred to as expectation values.

Applications¶

The inner product can be interpreted in many ways in Quantum Mechanics:

  • Quantum Overlap: Measures the similarity (or overlap) between two quantum states. If $|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 = 1$, the states are identical; if $|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 = 0$, they are orthogonal (completely distinct).

  • Probability Amplitudes: In measurement, the inner product $\langle i | \psi \rangle$ gives the probability amplitude of measuring state $\ket{\psi}$ in basis state $\ket{i}$. The probability is then $|\langle i | \psi \rangle|^2$.

  • Expectation Values: Computing the expected measurement outcome of an observable $\hat{O}$ in state $\ket{\psi}$ is given by $\langle \psi | \hat{O} | \psi \rangle$.

  • Orthonormality: Used to verify that basis states are orthonormal: $\langle i | j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta).

Outer Product¶

The reverse order produces a matrix (often used as an operator):

$$ \ket{\psi}\bra{\phi} = \begin{bmatrix} \psi{}_1 \\ \psi{}_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \phi{}_1^{*} & \phi{}_2^{*} \end{bmatrix} $$

Applications¶

  • Projection Operators: The outer product $\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}$ creates a projection operator that projects any state onto $\ket{\psi}$. This is fundamental in quantum measurement theory.

  • Density Matrices: A mixed quantum state can be represented as $\rho = \sum_i p_i \ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i}$, where $p_i$ are classical probabilities.

  • Gate Construction: Many quantum gates can be expressed using outer products. For example, the phase flip on $\ket{0}$ can be written as $I - 2\ket{0}\bra{0}$.

  • Completeness Relation: The identity operator can be decomposed as $I = \sum_i \ket{i}\bra{i}$ for a complete orthonormal basis $\{\ket{i}\}$.

Superposition¶

Unlike classical bits, a qubit can exist in a superposition of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, simultaneously. This is a probablilistic combination of all possible states until measured. (Think a linear combination of states.)

$$ \ket{\psi} = \alpha{} \ket{0} + \beta{}\ket{1} \qquad{} \alpha{}, \beta{} \in{} \mathbb{C} $$

Note that $\alpha{}$ and $\beta{}$ are complex amplitudes, and are NOT probabilities.

Superposition is the physical interpretation of a linear combination.

  • The qubit is not in state $\ket{0}$ or in state $\ket{1}$.
  • It instead exists in a coherent combination of both basis states.
  • Interference between amplitudes is possible (this enables many quantum algorithms to amplify correct answers)

Superposition enables quantum parallelism and interference, but is not inherently randomness.

Born Rule¶

The Born rule connects the quantum state to measurement outcomes:

$$ P(0) = |\alpha|^2, \qquad P(1) = |\beta|^2 $$

  • Measurement collapses the state to a basis state
  • Probabilites come from squared magnitudes.
  • It is important to ensure probabilites sum to 1.

We often want $ |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.

Alternatively,

$$ |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = \alpha \alpha^* + \beta \beta^* = 1 $$

Note how * denotes the complex conjugate of each value.

This is why superpositions often have square-rooted magnitudes.

Let's talk about gates, and what they do.

Identity matrix (I-Gate)¶

This applies the identity matrix to the state, appling the operation $I\ket{\psi}$ for some arbitrary state.

NOT Gate (Pauli-X Gate)¶

This applies the Pauli-X matrix to the state, inverting it's state.

$$ X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

H Gate (Hadamard Gate)¶

This applies the Hadamard Matrix to the state as a unitary operator. The Hadamard Matrix is defined as: $$ H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} $$ This puts a single qubit into an equal superposition in the computational basis.

Recall:

$$ \ket{0} \xrightarrow{H} \ket{+} \\ \ket{1} \xrightarrow{H} \ket{-} \\ $$

Key Definitions¶

  • Gate Time Definition: Time required to apply a single quantum gate operation

  • Optimization Balance: Balance operational speed against error rates and decoherence.

  • Decoherence Risk: Like any physical system, the QPU wants to return to it's state of least entropy. This is fundamentally bad for quantum computers, as it means the data we have stored will become unintelligible- as we have lost our information. (Usually a quantized energy level has entered an undefined energy level, or some other physical phenomena in other implementations of quantum systems)

This means we can lose our information stored in the system. This is further exacerbated by the introduction of additional quantum gates in our system, as they inherently introduce additional quantum noise into our states.

Unitary Operations¶

Unitary operations are incredibly important to quantum computing. Each operator is reversible.

For any unitary matrix $U$:

$$ UU^{\dagger} = I $$

  • We observe the conservation of probability (that is the total probability remains exactly 1),
  • We observe that all unitary operations (hence all quantum operations) can be undone.

To check if a matrix is unitary, verify that matrix multiplicaiton with it's complex conjugate yields the identity matrix.

In short, Unitary Matrices preserve probabilites and are reversible.

Note that matrices are linear operators. Therefore all properties of linearity apply.

Why are matrices called operators?¶

Matrices act on vectors to produce new vectors - acting exactly like functions that transform inputs to outputs.

We can apply these operations sequentially, as defined already in matrix arithmetic.

Using matrices as operators unlocks eigenvalue analysis, diagonalization and spectral theory - tools for solving complex problems.

The below equation shows that, for any arbitrary normalized axis $\hat{n}$, we can perform a rotation around it, by generating a unitary operator, denoted $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})$. Note that $\vec{\sigma_{n}}$ denotes the vector definition for the axis $\hat{n}$:

The below excerpt is adapted from my previous coursework for PHYS223:

a) The identity is resolved as follows:

$$ \begin{align} R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{}) &= e^{-i \frac{\theta{}}{2}\hat{n}\cdot{}\vec{\sigma{}}} \tag{Given by (1)} \\ &\equiv{} \sum{}_{k = 0}^{\infty{}} \left(\frac{\left(i \frac{\theta}{2} \vec{\sigma_{n}} \right)^{k}}{k!} \right) \tag{via the Maclaurin series expansion of $e^x$.} \\ &\equiv{} \sum{}_{k = 0}^{\infty{}} \left(\frac{\left(i \frac{\theta}{2} \vec{\sigma_{n}} \right)^{(2k)}}{(2k)!} \right) + \sum{}_{k = 0}^{\infty{}} \left(\frac{\left(i \frac{\theta}{2} \vec{\sigma_{n}} \right)^{(2k+1)}}{(2k + 1)!} \right) \tag{splitting into even and odd sums.} \\ \end{align} $$

As $\vec{\sigma{}_n}$ is a unit vector, we know that $\vec{\sigma{}_n}^2 = 1$ (This is because any unit vector can be formed as a linear combination of $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$). (We also use the properties of $i$ to simplify further.) Therefore, we can simplify the Maclaurin series:

$$ \begin{align} &\equiv{} \sum{}_{k = 0}^{\infty{}} \left(\frac{\left((-1)^{k} \left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{2k} \right)}{(2k)!} \cdot I \right) + \sum{}_{k = 0}^{\infty{}} \left(\frac{\left(-i (-1)^k \left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{2k + 1} \right)}{(2k + 1)!} \cdot{} \vec{\sigma_{n}} \right) \\ &\equiv{} \cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I - i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \qquad{} \tag{By expressing the Maclaurin series in closed form.} \end{align} $$ b) To prove that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})$ is unitary, we must show that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{\dagger} = R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{-1}$, which implies that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{\dagger} \cdot R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{}) = I$.

Hence, we must resolve the identity:

First, we have that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{\dagger} = \left(\cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I - i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \right)^{\dagger} = \cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I + i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n}$.

This holds as both the identity and the Pauli matrices are Hermitian.

Next, we have that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{\dagger} \cdot R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{}) = \left(\cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I + i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \right) \cdot \left(\cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I - i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \right) = \cos^{2}{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I + \sin^{2}{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot{} I = I$.

As we have just resolved the identity, we have shown that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})$ is unitary.

Next, we can show that $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^\dagger = R_{\hat{n}}(-\theta{})$.

This may be achieved by subbing in $-\theta$ into the expression for $R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})$.

$$ \begin{align} R_{\hat{n}}(-\theta{}) &= \cos{\frac{-\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I - i \sin{\frac{-\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \\ &= \cos{\frac{\theta{}}{2}} \cdot{} I + i \sin{\frac{\theta}{2}} \cdot \vec{\sigma_n} \\ &= R_{\hat{n}}(\theta{})^{\dagger} \end{align} $$

2-qubit systems¶

These systems consist of 2 qubits instead of just one. $$ \ket{\psi} = \ket{\phi{}_1} \otimes \ket{\phi{}_2} = \ket{\phi{}_1 \phi{}_2} $$

This allows us to use controlled (mutli-qubit) gates, like CNOT.

As an example:

$$ \ket{\psi{}_1} = \alpha{}\beta{}\ket{10} = \alpha{}\ket{1} \otimes \beta{}\ket{0} = \alpha{}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \otimes \beta{}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \alpha{}\beta{}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ 1 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix} = \alpha{}\beta{}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} $$

Therefore we can observe that Dirac notation is a short-hand format for tensor-product form, where a bitstring within a dirac state is equal to each individual bit being tensor-multiplied with each surrounding bit in order.

$$ \ket{abc} = \ket{a} \otimes \ket{b} \otimes \ket{c} $$

CNOT (among other multi-qubit gates)¶

Multi-qubit gates are larger than $2 \times 2$ matrices. This is because they operate on vectors of a higher dimensionality, and therefore must be larger to accomodate this. Given $n$ qubits to operate on, they will be of size $2^n \times 2^n$.

In general, if we are repeating the same gate $n$ times, we can use the notation: $$ U^{\otimes n} = U \otimes U \otimes U \otimes \dots \otimes U $$

Interference¶

Amplitudes can interfere constructively or deconstructively, because they are complex numbers.

Constructive: Amplitudes with the same phase add together and increase the probability. Deconstructive: Amplitudes with opposing phases subtract and reduce the probability.

(This kind of works like the summation of sinusoidal functions)

Entanglement¶

Qubits that should appear independent often influence other (entangled) qubits when they are affected. This means we shouldn't consider independent probabilities, but instead calculate probability distributions.

Changing the direction of one qubit changes all the dependent probabilities.

def generate_arbitrary_state(alpha, beta, gamma, delta):
    """
    Generates a circuit U such that U|00> = a|00> + b|01> + c|10> + d|11>
    using manual rotation decomposition.
    """
    
    # 1. VALIDATION & NORMALIZATION
    coeffs = np.array([alpha, beta, gamma, delta], dtype=complex)
    norm = np.linalg.norm(coeffs)
    
    if not np.isclose(norm, 1.0):
        # Normalize the coefficients
        coeffs = coeffs / norm
        alpha, beta, gamma, delta = coeffs

        
    # 2. CALCULATE PROBABILITIES FOR Q0
    # Group coefficients by the state of Q0
    # Branch 0 (Q0=0): coefficients alpha, beta
    p0 = np.abs(alpha)**2 + np.abs(beta)**2
    
    # Branch 1 (Q0=1): coefficients gamma, delta
    p1 = np.abs(gamma)**2 + np.abs(delta)**2
    
    # Calculate Theta for Q0 Rotation (Ry)
    # Ry(theta) |0> = cos(theta/2)|0> + sin(theta/2)|1>
    # We want cos(theta/2) = sqrt(p0)
    theta_root = 2 * np.arccos(np.sqrt(p0))
    
    # 3. HELPER: CONVERT VECTOR TO U-GATE ANGLES
    # We need a function to turn a target vector [c0, c1] into U(theta, phi, lambda)
    def get_u_params(c0, c1):
        # Normalize local branch
        local_norm = np.sqrt(np.abs(c0)**2 + np.abs(c1)**2)
        if local_norm == 0: 
            return 0, 0, 0, 0 # Dead branch
            
        c0 = c0 / local_norm
        c1 = c1 / local_norm
        
        # Convert to Polar
        r0, phase0 = cmath.polar(c0)
        r1, phase1 = cmath.polar(c1)
        
        # U(theta, phi, lambda) * |0> = cos(theta/2)|0> + e^i(phi)*sin(theta/2)|1>
        # The U gate creates a real coefficient for |0>.
        # We must track the 'global phase' of this branch (phase0) to apply later.
        
        theta = 2 * np.arccos(r0)
        phi = phase1 - phase0
        lam = 0 # Doesn't affect output when starting from |0>
        
        return theta, phi, lam, phase0

    # Calculate parameters for the two branches
    theta0, phi0, lam0, phase_branch0 = get_u_params(alpha, beta)
    theta1, phi1, lam1, phase_branch1 = get_u_params(gamma, delta)

    # 4. CONSTRUCT THE CIRCUIT
    qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
    
    # A. Root Rotation on Q0
    qc.ry(theta_root, 0)
    
    # B. Phase Correction on Q0
    # We need to fix the relative phase between the |0...> branch and |1...> branch
    # The U-gates we are about to apply assume the first coeff is real, 
    # but 'alpha' and 'gamma' might have phases.
    relative_phase = phase_branch1 - phase_branch0
    qc.p(relative_phase, 0) # Apply phase difference to Q1 branch (controlled by Q0=1)
    
    # Optional: If we cared about Global Phase, we would apply phase_branch0 globally.
    
    # C. Multiplexed Rotations on Q1
    # We apply U_0 if Q0 is 0, and U_1 if Q0 is 1.
    
    # Branch 0: Control-0 (Open Control)
    u_gate0 = UGate(theta0, phi0, lam0)
    # Using 'ctrl_state=0' makes it activate when control qubit is 0
    c_u0 = u_gate0.control(1, ctrl_state=0) 
    qc.append(c_u0, [0, 1])
    
    # Branch 1: Control-1 (Standard Control)
    u_gate1 = UGate(theta1, phi1, lam1)
    c_u1 = u_gate1.control(1, ctrl_state=1)
    qc.append(c_u1, [0, 1])
    
    return qc

Oracles, Deustch, Speedup and Phase Kickback¶

What is an oracle?¶

An oracle is a function that returns an answer-- such as yes or no. The implementation and logic of the function is unknown to the user.

In other words, an oracle is a black box.

Given a boolean function $f$, where we are not given the definition, but have the possibility to "probe", or submit an input, and recieve an output, we call such a function an oracle.

Our objective could be to learn some property of $f$, or some other task.

Taking this into the quantum realm, we can consider this function to be some arbitrary unitary. At it's simplest, it will be a 2-qubit unitary, accepting $x$ and $y$, returning $x$ and $f(x) \oplus y$. We consider $f(x) \oplus y$ to be the oracle response.

Deustch Problem¶

Given an unknown function $f : \{0, 1 \} \leftarrow \{0, 1 \}$, we want to be able to probe the function and determine if it is constant or balanced. In classical computing, we must probe the entire domain of the function in order to analyze it's co-domain (and come up with an answer). However, we can do better in quantum computing.

Quantum Algorithm Design¶

Before we can design quantum algorithms, we need to review the basic building blocks of quantum computation, and the tools we have at our disposal.

Complex Phase and Ampliude¶

Euler's Formula¶

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos{\theta} + i \sin{\theta} $$

Conjugate Form¶

$$ e^{-i\theta} = \cos{\theta} - i \sin{\theta} $$

Cosine and Sine in Terms of Exponentials¶

$$ \cos{\theta} = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2} $$ $$ \sin{\theta} = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i} $$

Note that any sine function can be expressed as a phase-shifted cosine function, and vice versa. $$ \sin{\theta} = \cos{\left(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)} $$

Polar Form of Complex Numbers¶

Any complex number can be expressed in polar form as: $$ z = r e^{i\theta} $$ or in trigonometric form as: $$ z = r (\cos{\theta} + i \sin{\theta}) $$ Where $r$ is the magnitude (or modulus) of the complex number, and $\theta$ is the argument (or angle) of the complex number.

  • $e^{i\pi/2} = i$
  • $e^{i\pi} = -1$
  • $e^{i3\pi/2} = -i$

Core Story of a Quantum Algorithm¶

Classical Computing Quantum Computing
Bits are 0 or 1 Qubits can be in superposition of 0 and 1
Computation manipulates bits directly Computation manipulates probability amplitudes and phases of qubits
Measurement is deterministic and reads the state of bits Measurement is probabilistic and collapses qubits to a basis state

Key differences:

  • Quantum algorithms leverage superposition and interference to explore multiple solutions simultaneously.
  • Quantum algorithms often require fewer steps than classical algorithms for certain problems (e.g., Shor's algorithm for factoring).
  • Quantum algorithms can solve problems that are intractable for classical computers (e.g., simulating quantum systems).

Crucial Difference: Classical algorithms manipulate bits directly, while quantum algorithms manipulate the probability amplitudes and phases of qubits, allowing for phenomena like superposition and interference that enable quantum speedups.

State: Values vs. Amplitudes¶

In classical computing, the state of a system is represented by bits that can be either 0 or 1 (a switch). In quantum computing, the state of a system is represented by qubits that can be in a superposition of 0 and 1, described by complex probability amplitudes (acts like a wave).

  • Classical Bits: Exist in a discrete state. If you have n bits, the system can be in one of $2^n$ possible states, but only one at a time.
  • Quantum Qubits: Exist in a continuous superposition (a linear combination) of states. The complex amplitudes determine the probability of measuring a particular state, and can interfere with each other, leading to constructive or destructive interference.
    • For a qubit in a state $\ket{\psi} = \alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1}$, the probabilities of measuring 0 or 1 are given by $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ respectively, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers.
    • Remember that the probabilities must sum to 1, so $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.

Operation: Logic vs. Interference¶

  • Classical computing is all about "flipping switches" (applying logic gates, like AND, XOR, NOT to bits). The operations are deterministic and directly manipulate the state of the bits.

  • Quantum computing is all about "manipulating waves" (applying unitary transformations to qubits). The operations are reversible and can create superpositions and entanglement, allowing for interference effects that can amplify correct solutions and cancel out incorrect ones.

  • Quantum Gates: Unitary transformations (rotations on the Bloch Sphere). By manipulating the phases of the probability amplitudes, quantum algorithms can create interference patterns that enhance the probability of measuring the correct answer.

    • Constructive Interference: When the phases of the amplitudes align, they add together, increasing the probability of measuring that state.
    • Destructive Interference: When the phases of the amplitudes are opposite, they cancel each other out, reducing the probability of measuring that state.

Result: Reading vs. Collapsing¶

The way we extract information in quantum computing is the most counterintuitive difference from classical computing.

  • Classical Computing: Reading the state of bits is straightforward and deterministic. The bits are in a definite state (0 or 1), and reading them simply reveals that state without altering it.

  • Quantum Computing: Measurement collapses the superposition of qubits into a definite state. You don't see the complex amplitudes directly; instead, you get a probabilistic outcome based on the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes, forcing the system to assume one of the basis states.

    • The probability of measuring a specific state is the squared magnitude of its amplitude. For example, if $\ket{\psi} = \alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1}$, the probability of measuring 0 is $|\alpha|^2$ and the probability of measuring 1 is $|\beta|^2$.
    • Each amplitude $\alpha_k = r_k e^{i\theta_k}$ has a magnitude $r_k$ that determines the probability of measuring that state, and a phase $\theta_k$ that can interfere with other states.

  • Measurement ignores the phase information, so the outcome is purely probabilistic based on the magnitudes of the amplitudes. This is a fundamental aspect of quantum mechanics and is what allows quantum algorithms to achieve speedups over classical algorithms for certain problems.

  • To leverage phase, we use unitary gates to generate interference patterns that amplify the probability of correct answers and suppress incorrect ones, which is a key strategy in quantum algorithm design.

Measuring Complex Amplitudes¶

When we measure, we read out probabilities-- achieved by multipliying an amplitude by it's complex conjugate:

  • Before measurement, amplitude is squared algebraically: $$ \alpha_k^2 = \left( r_k e^{i\theta_k} \right)^2 \implies \alpha_k^2 = r_k^2 e^{2i\theta_k} $$

  • Observe that the phase is doubled and the magnitude is squared, while the value remains complex.

  • After measurement, we get a real number (the probability), which is the squared magnitude of the amplitude: $$ \alpha_k = r_k e^{i\theta_k} \quad \Rightarrow \quad |\alpha_k|^2 = \alpha_k \alpha_k^* = r_k^2 $$

  • We have some facts about measurement:

    • The phase is cancelled out, and destroys quantum information.
    • However, we can use this magnitude as a probability, which is inherently defined to be a real number between 0 and 1. (At this point this becomes classical information)
    • The amplitude collapses into a real-world probability.

Quantum Algorithms¶

Step 1: Create Parallel Computing Paths¶

We can do this by putting all the qubits into superposition, which allows us to explore all possible inputs simultaneously. We do this by applying the Hadamard gate to each qubit, which creates an equal superposition of all possible states.

For a single qubit: $$ H\ket{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{0} + \ket{1}) $$

For two qubits: $$ H^{\otimes 2} \ket{00} = \frac{1}{2} (\ket{00} + \ket{01} + \ket{10} + \ket{11}) $$

For many (n) qubits: $$ H^{\otimes n} \ket{0}^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} \ket{x} $$ We can simplify this by pulling out a $(-1)^{x \cdot y}$ term, which allows us to express the state as a sum over all possible inputs, with a phase factor that depends on the input. $$ H^{\otimes n} \ket{y}^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} (-1)^{x \cdot y} \ket{x} $$

  • Creates the level quantum horizon
  • Creates parallel computing paths, which is the first step in achieving quantum speedup.
  • Amplitudes have equal magnitude
  • All phases are aligned

Step 2: Encode the Problem into the Phase¶

Oracle or Phase Encoding¶

  • Two types of problem encoding:
    1. Boolean Oracle: Encodes the problem into the phase of the quantum state, allowing us to leverage interference to amplify correct answers and suppress incorrect ones.
    2. Hamiltonian Encoding: Encodes the problem into the energy levels of a quantum system, which can be used for algorithms like Quantum Phase Estimation and Quantum Simulation.
  • Pauli Gates, mutli-controlled phase gates and other unitary gates can be used to encode the problem into the phase of the quantum state.

Boolean Oracle¶

  • Used for search algorithms and decision problems.
  • Encodes a function $f(x)$ into the phase of the quantum state, such that $$ U_f \ket{x} = (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x} $$
  • This allows us to create interference patterns that amplify the probability of measuring the correct answer, without revealing the function itself.
  • Essentially, this provides a mathematical description of "finding the needle in a haystack".

Hamiltonian Encoding¶

  • Used for problems in quantum simulation and optimization.
  • Encode teh contraints of a molecule or a system into a mathematical operator called a Hamiltonian, which describes the energy of the system.
  • The phases accumulate at different speeds depending on the energy of each state.
  • We aim to find the lowest energy state, which corresponds to the optimal solution of the problem.

Key point: Step 2 does not affect amplitudes, but only the phases. This is crucial for leveraging interference in the next step.

Step 3: Interference Reshapes the Problem into Phase¶

  • Apply Hadamard gates first to rotate the landscape back into a state where we can manipulate the average of the amplitudes.

Our phase changes that we made are now perceptible as we shift back to a measureable basis.

We have a diffusion operator with three distinct layers. Think of this like a "a sandwich of gates" or "applying mixing gates".

Layer 1: Horizon - Hadamard¶

Layer 2: Marker - Inversion Filter¶

  • Inversion filter - use Pauli X gates on all qubits so that teh all-zero state represents the mean; the average is now the pivot point for the inversion.

Layer 3: Reshape - Multi-Controlled Phase Gates¶

  • Apply a phase flip (Z gate) to one specific state.
    • This has the effect of creating mathematical interference, which "identifies the needle in the haystack".
  • Repeat the X and H gates to return the landscape to the original basis, but with the amplitudes reshaped by the interference.

Step 4: Measure the State and Extract the Engineered Bias¶

  • Measurement DOES NOT COMPUTE the answer, but instead reads out the probabilities of the engineered state.
  • This reveals the result of the interference pattern we applied.

At this point, desired states now have large amplitudes, while undesired states have small amplitudes.

The Universal Quantum Algorithm Template¶

Superposition: Create many parallel computing paths by putting qubits into superposition.

Phase Encoding: Write the porblem into the phase of the quantum state, without affecting the amplitudes.

Interference: Mix amplitudes to reshape the probability landscape, amplifying correct answers and suppressing incorrect ones.

Measurement: Sample from the engineered probability distribution to extract the answer.

Quantum computing gets it's speedup from precisely engineering the interference patterns to amplify the probability of correct answers, while suppressing incorrect ones. This is achieved through careful design of the quantum circuit, leveraging superposition and phase manipulation to create constructive interference for the desired solution.

In short, a quantum algorithm is a carefully engineerd sequence of unitary operations that encodes problem structure into relative phases, and then uses interference to transform thoses phases into amplitude differences, and then extracts the result through measurement.

This reminds me a lot of ECE310. In signals and systems, the DFT rewrites a discrete-time signal in terms of complex exponential basis functions, so we can analyze the signal by its frequency content instead of by its samples in time. The Fourier basis in quantum computing feels very similar: instead of rewriting a time-domain signal, we rewrite a quantum state’s amplitude vector in a phase/frequency basis. So, in both courses, the Fourier basis is really a change of basis that makes periodic structure easier to see.

In short: the QFT is basically a change of basis that makes periodic information easy to read.

Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)¶

QPE starts with an eigenstate $\ket{\psi}$ of a unitary $U$:

$$ U\ket{\psi} = e^{2\pi i\phi}\ket{\psi}. $$

The goal is to estimate the phase $\phi$.

The algorithm, at a high level, is:

  1. Put the counting register into equal superposition.
  2. Apply controlled powers $U, U^2, U^4, \dots$.
  3. Use phase kickback to write the phase information onto the counting register.
  4. Apply the inverse QFT.
  5. Measure an approximation of the binary digits of $\phi$.

What finally clicked for me is that the inverse QFT is not creating the answer. After the controlled powers, the counting register is already in a Fourier-type phase state; the inverse QFT just decodes it into an ordinary bit string.

A useful intermediate picture is

$$ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^m}} \sum_{k=0}^{2^m-1} e^{2\pi i k\phi}\ket{k}, $$

which is exactly the kind of phase pattern the inverse QFT knows how to decode.

If the unitary is built from a Hamiltonian, $U = e^{-itH}$, then estimating phase becomes estimating energy. That is why QPE keeps coming back in chemistry and simulation.

QUBO and Ising form¶

A lot of hard classical problems can be written using binary decision variables. The examples in these slides were things like MaxCut, Vertex Cover, Graph Colouring, and Dominating Set.

The standard QUBO form is

$$ f_Q(x) = x^T Q x, \qquad x_i \in \{0,1\}. $$

If the original problem has constraints, the trick is to fold them into the objective with penalty terms. So invalid solutions are still present mathematically, but they become too expensive to be attractive.

Then we convert binary variables into spin variables:

$$ x_i = \frac{1-z_i}{2}, \qquad z_i \in \{-1,1\}. $$

After promoting the $z_i$ to Pauli-$Z$ operators, we get a cost Hamiltonian $H_C$ satisfying

$$ H_C\ket{x} = C(x)\ket{x}. $$

Because $H_C$ is diagonal in the computational basis, exponentiating it just attaches cost-dependent phase:

$$ e^{-itH_C}\ket{x} = e^{-itC(x)}\ket{x}. $$

That was a really nice moment for me, because it tied optimization back to the same “encode useful structure into phase” chain of thought from earlier lectures.

QAOA¶

QAOA alternates a cost Hamiltonian with a mixer Hamiltonian:

$$ \ket{\psi_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})} = \left( \prod_{j=1}^{p} e^{-i\beta_j H_M} e^{-i\gamma_j H_C} \right) H^{\otimes n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}. $$

My understanding of the pieces is:

  • $H_C$ marks good and bad solutions through phase.
  • $H_M$ moves amplitude around the search space so we do not get stuck on one basis state.
  • A classical optimizer tunes the parameters to lower the expected cost.

The lectures also made the hardware distinction clearer:

  • annealers are very natural for QUBO / Ising formulations,
  • gate-based machines use circuits built from cost and mixer layers.

  1. In your own words, describe the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) algorithm. How is QFT used in quantum computing?

The QFT algorithm is a change of basis that takes a quantum state from the computational basis to the Fourier basis. It works by generating a superposition of all possible states, and rotating them in a specific way to achieve the correct phases (which encode the frequency information). We apply a swap gates at the end to ensure the correct ordering. This allows us to analyze periodic structure in the state, which is useful for algorithms like Shor's algorithm for factoring and Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE). This can also be extended to a field of applications in signal processing, where the QFT can be used to analyze the frequency content of a quantum state.

This allows us to extract information about the eigenvalues of the operator, which can be used to solve various problems.

  1. What is the role of eigenvalues and eigenstates (eigenvectors) in quantum computing?

The Eigenstructure of a problem are central to quantum computing. The Eigenstates are the "natural" states of a system, and the eigenvalues tell us how those states evolve under certain operations. Eigenstates are the "natural" basis states of a linear operator, and the associated eigenvalues tell us how the operator acts on those states.

We can take this idea further when considering Hermitian operators, which have the eigenvalues as a "list" of potential measurement outcomes. QPE uses this directly to estimate the eigenvalues of a unitary operator, and QAOA uses the eigenvalues of the cost Hamiltonian to guide the optimization process.
